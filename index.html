Thermodynamics Behind F1 Engines

How are thermodynamic principles used in Formula One engines to optimise speed?

Abstract

This essay explores how thermodynamic principles are utilised in Formula One power units to enhance vehicle speed. FIA regulations prohibit teams from increasing vehicle performance from just burning more fuel as fuel flow was restricted to 100kg/h. Therefore, engineers must maximise the conversion of chemical energy to useful mechanical work. The first law of thermodynamics presents the engine as a system that converts energy, while the second law of thermodynamics explains why waste heat is unavoidable and encourages heat recovery. This essay examines how high compression ratios, lean combustion, pre-chamber jet ignition, MGU-H and MGU-K energy recovery systems increase thermal efficiency. It considers 2026 regulations, which remove the MGU-H, increase MGU-K power to 350kW and require sustainable fuel usage. These technologies utilise thermodynamic principles to achieve thermal efficiency, resulting in greater acceleration, straight-line speed and faster lap times from the same amount of fuel. (Global.honda, 2015)

Introduction

Thermodynamics is the study of the movement of heat in macroscopic objects (Devriendt, 2021) and is imperative when considering how to enhance speed in F1 vehicles. F1 cars can achieve high speeds due to high thermal efficiency of the power units, meaning a limited quantity of fuel energy converts into useful mechanical work whilst limiting waste heat. To be environmentally conscious, a maximum fuel flow was set, prompting engineers to enhance thermal efficiency. F1 engines achieve thermal efficiency by applying laws of thermodynamics, optimising combustion and recovering waste heat. (Menon, 2026)

First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law of thermodynamics states energy is conserved, it can change form but cannot be created nor destroyed. In F1 engines, the fuel’s chemical energy is released during combustion and converted into thermal energy, pressure energy, mechanical work and waste heat. The useful energy is transferred to the crankshaft, which drives the wheels. (Global.honda, 2015c) Maximum fuel flow means the engine cannot burn more fuel to increase speed. Therefore, useful energy transferred to the crankshaft and rear wheels must be optimised to maximise speed by prioritising thermal efficiency. This is expressed as: η_thermal =( W_useful)/E_fuel Therefore, increasing useful energy output increases thermal efficiency. This is done by reducing energy losses through improved combustion and recovering exhaust energy. Mechanical work is used to overcome aerodynamic drag and rolling resistance. Therefore, increased efficiency in converting fuel energy into work allows greater acceleration, straight-line speed and less fuel usage.

Second Law of Thermodynamics

The second law of thermodynamics suggests an engine cannot convert all its heat into useful work because entropy, a system’s randomised state of energy, always increases. (thinka, 2026) Therefore, some energy released during combustion leaves through the exhaust meaning engineers must reduce losses and recover wasted energy. This recovered energy can be used as additional power increasing efficiency and speed.

Energy Recovery Systems

The MGU-K, used during breaking, is an electric motor with permanent magnets mechanically connected to the crankshaft. It captures kinetic energy, converting it into electrical energy. During acceleration this energy is converted into mechanical energy to supply the drivetrain. The MGU-H is a permanent magnet AC electric motor mechanically connected to the turbocharger, where exhaust gas entropy (“thermal energy remaining in the exhaust gas as a function of its temperature, expansion and mass flow rate”) (Stepien, 2025). It rotates the turbine during the energy recovery phase, converting rotational energy from exhaust gases into electrical energy. This operates continuously to maintain engine efficiency. The Battery and Control Electronics system manages energy flow between MGU-H and MGU-K. A lithium-ion battery stores recovered energy from breaking and exhaust gases, then releases 120kW during acceleration improving thermal efficiency. Turbocharged engines use vacuum pulsation control and the Miller cycle, which cool the intake air through adiabatic expansion, reducing knock. The Miller cycle causes the delay of intake valve closure which separates the expansion ratio from the compression ratio. This helps reduce combustion knock whilst maintaining high expansion/compression ratios causing greater gas expansion and thermal efficiency. (Stepien, 2025)

Kinetic Energy Recovery Systems (KERS)

KERS, shown in figure 2. (Formula 1 car energy recovery system diagram, n.d.) stores energy from breaking and converts it into electrical energy causing temporary power increases. During breaking, kinetic energy is lost as thermal energy in the brake discs. However, with KERS, kinetic energy is converted into electrical energy, which is stored and released during acceleration, increasing efficiency. (Stepien, 2025)

Pre-Chamber Jet Combustion

This system has a pre-chamber containing an air-fuel mixture around the spark plug. (Global.honda, 2015b) During ignition, combustion products pass small nozzles into the main chamber, producing high energy flames which ignite lean air-fuel mixtures causing faster combustion and higher compression ratios. This improves thermal efficiency by reducing waste heat and producing useful energy output. Higher compression ratios, (a measure for how much the air-fuel mixture is compressed before ignition), increase the temperature and pressure of the air-fuel mixture before ignition, enabling greater energy extraction during expansion resulting in greater cylinder pressure, force on the piston and higher engine power. (SAE Media Group, 2016) However, higher compression ratios lead to combustion knock, where the mixture temperature is too high causing self-ignition and power loss. Rapid flame prorogation increases cylinder pressure, causing increased force on the piston and higher mechanical power. Therefore, this technology improves the conversion of chemical energy into mechanical energy while reducing energy loss. (Propulsion and Energy Lab - Purdue University, n.d.)

Conclusion

The First Law of Thermodynamics explains how the fuel’s chemical energy is converted into thermal energy and mechanical work causing wheel rotation. The Second Law suggests energy will be lost as heat therefore, maximising efficiency requires mitigating losses. Technologies such as, pre-chamber jet combustion, MGU-K and MGU-H improve combustion efficiency, increase compression ratios, recover energy and convert more fuel energy into useful work. Fuel regulations limit energy for combustion, so increasing thermal efficiency is vital to increase engine power. Hence, applying thermodynamic principes allows power units to achieve thermal efficiency, allowing greater acceleration and straight-line speed.
